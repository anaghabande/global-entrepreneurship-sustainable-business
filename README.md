# Global Entrepreneurship & Sustainable Business

## Overview

This repository presents applied work in global entrepreneurship, venture development, financial inclusion, sustainability strategy, and responsible business.

The portfolio contains 2 major projects:

1. **Pearl Credit Union Bank**, a proposed financial inclusion venture designed for underserved consumers and informal sector participants in Zimbabwe.

2. **Wild Leather**, a sustainability strategy project examining biodiversity loss, exotic leather supply chains, invasive species, stakeholder tensions, and enterprise responses.

Together, these projects demonstrate the integration of entrepreneurship, finance, customer discovery, sustainability, systems thinking, supply chain analysis, and strategic decision making.

# Project 1: Pearl Credit Union Bank

## Venture Overview

**Pearl Credit Union Bank, or PCUB**, is a proposed member owned financial institution designed to expand access to formal financial services in Zimbabwe.

The venture focuses on people who generate real economic activity but remain excluded or underserved by traditional financial institutions because of collateral requirements, formal employment documentation, fees, limited credit histories, or other structural barriers.

The proposed model combines digital banking, alternative credit assessment, physical kiosks, and member ownership to create a more accessible financial ecosystem.

## Problem Identified

Zimbabwe has a substantial informal economy, yet many economically active individuals and small businesses face difficulty accessing traditional financial services.

PCUB addresses several challenges:

• Limited access to formal credit  
• Dependence on collateral and payslips  
• High banking fees  
• Limited formal credit histories  
• Financial exclusion among informal workers  
• Limited access to working capital  
• Trust challenges between consumers and financial institutions  
• Limited financial products designed around informal economic activity  

The project therefore asks a fundamental entrepreneurial question:

**How can a regulated financial institution redesign banking around the actual financial behavior of underserved customers?**

---

## Value Proposition

PCUB is built around 3 central principles.

### Zero Fee Everyday Banking

The proposed model eliminates many routine banking fees to reduce barriers to financial participation.

### Behavior Based Credit

Instead of relying exclusively on traditional documentation and collateral, PCUB proposes evaluating customers through observable financial behavior.

Potential indicators include:

• Income consistency  
• Transaction frequency  
• Savings activity  
• Repayment history  
• Spending behavior  
• Account activity  

The objective is to allow customers to progressively establish financial credibility through their actual behavior.

### Digital First Access

The venture combines mobile access with strategically located physical kiosks.

Customers would be able to access services through:

• Mobile devices  
• QR codes  
• Digital payments  
• Community kiosks  
• Payment cards  
• Mobile money integration  

This branch light structure is designed to reduce operating costs while maintaining physical accessibility where customers already conduct economic activity.

---

## Customer Segments

The venture identifies multiple customer segments across Zimbabwe's financial ecosystem.

### Informal Sector Workers

The primary segment includes economically active individuals who may generate regular income without conventional employment documentation.

Examples include:

• Market traders  
• Street vendors  
• Transport operators  
• Resellers  
• Welders  
• Manufacturers  
• Small service providers  
• Micro entrepreneurs  

### Salaried & Public Sector Workers

PCUB also considers formally employed but underserved consumers, including:

• Teachers  
• Nurses  
• Government employees  
• Entry level workers  
• Private sector employees  

These customers may already have access to banking but still experience high fees, limited credit availability, or inadequate financial products.

### Diaspora

The venture also considers Zimbabweans living abroad as a potential strategic customer segment.

Diaspora customers could support:

• Remittances  
• Savings  
• Credit products  
• Mortgage products  
• Cross border financial relationships  

### Students & Young Adults

Students and young adults represent an opportunity to establish customer relationships early in their financial lives.

Providing accessible financial products can allow these customers to begin developing formal financial histories before entering full time employment.

---

## Credit Progression Model

PCUB proposes a progressive lending structure in which customers gain access to larger and more sophisticated financial products as their financial histories develop.

### Stage 1

Small loans designed to establish repayment behavior.

### Stage 2

Personal loans and credit products linked to demonstrated repayment consistency.

### Stage 3

Working capital financing for small businesses and entrepreneurs.

### Stage 4

Longer term financial products such as mortgages.

The underlying principle is:

**Customers should be able to build creditworthiness through financial behavior rather than being permanently excluded because they lack traditional documentation.**

---

## Customer Discovery

Customer discovery formed an important component of the venture development process.

Interviews were used to test assumptions about how potential customers interact with financial institutions.

### Key Insights

Customer discovery highlighted several recurring concerns:

• Trust is a major barrier to banking adoption  
• Fees discourage engagement  
• Mobile money is deeply embedded in everyday transactions  
• Access to credit remains difficult  
• Customers value access to physical cash  
• Simple financial products are important  
• USD value protection matters  
• Trust develops gradually through consistent experience  

These findings influenced the refinement of the venture concept.

### Changes After Customer Discovery

The initial concept placed significant emphasis on low fees and digital banking.

Customer interviews indicated that the venture should place greater emphasis on:

• Trust  
• Cash accessibility  
• Simplicity  
• Financial security  
• USD value protection  
• Consistent service  

This illustrates an important entrepreneurial principle:

**Customer discovery should change the venture when market evidence challenges initial assumptions.**

---

## Go to Market Strategy

PCUB's proposed go to market strategy is based on physical proximity, trust building, and integration with existing financial behavior.

### Community Kiosks

Low cost kiosks would be positioned in high activity economic locations where target customers already transact.

### Payroll Partnerships

Partnerships with employers and payroll systems could provide an efficient customer acquisition channel for salaried workers.

### Mobile First Engagement

Digital communication could use platforms already familiar to customers, including:

• WhatsApp  
• SMS  
• Facebook  
• Mobile banking  

### University Activation

University based customer acquisition could introduce students and young adults to formal financial services earlier in their financial journeys.

### Community Referrals

Word of mouth and peer validation can be particularly important in environments where institutional trust is limited.

The strategy therefore emphasizes customer experience and community credibility rather than relying exclusively on traditional advertising.

---

## Competitive Advantage

PCUB's proposed competitive positioning combines several elements:

### Financial Inclusion

Serving customers overlooked by traditional banking models.

### Alternative Credit Assessment

Using behavioral information to evaluate customers with limited formal credit histories.

### Low Cost Infrastructure

Using mobile access and kiosks instead of relying heavily on traditional branches.

### Member Ownership

The proposed credit union structure aligns the institution more directly with its members.

### Data Driven Lending

Transaction and repayment behavior can gradually improve credit assessment as the customer base grows.

---

## Minimum Viable Product

The proposed early stage implementation focuses on testing the concept before large scale expansion.

### Months 1 to 2

• Launch a basic mobile platform  
• Establish initial test kiosks  
• Develop onboarding processes  

### Months 3 to 4

• Recruit early customers  
• Test small loans  
• Gather transaction and repayment information  

### Months 5 to 6

• Improve behavioral credit scoring  
• Refine customer experience  
• Evaluate customer feedback  
• Expand selectively based on validated demand  

This approach emphasizes experimentation and learning before aggressive scaling.

# Project 2: Wild Leather

## Biodiversity, Luxury & Sustainable Supply Chains

The Wild Leather project examines a complex sustainability challenge at the intersection of luxury fashion, biodiversity, wildlife conservation, global supply chains, consumer demand, and ethical sourcing.

The analysis evaluates whether enterprises can transform the environmental challenge associated with exotic leather into an opportunity for more sustainable innovation.

---

## The Sustainability Challenge

Exotic leather remains economically and culturally significant within luxury markets.

At the same time, exotic leather supply chains create or interact with several environmental and governance challenges:

• Biodiversity loss  
• Illegal wildlife trade  
• Weak enforcement  
• Animal welfare concerns  
• Supply chain opacity  
• Consumer demand for scarcity  
• Regulatory exposure  
• Reputational risk  
• Climate related ecosystem disruption  

The project therefore treats exotic leather as a **wicked problem** rather than a simple sourcing problem.

---

## Wicked Problem Analysis

A wicked problem involves multiple interconnected causes, competing stakeholder priorities, uncertainty, and solutions that may create additional consequences.

The project identifies several important drivers.

### Luxury Status & Scarcity

Scarcity can increase the perceived value of exotic materials, creating continued demand for rare products.

### Governance & Enforcement

Differences in enforcement capabilities across countries can create opportunities for illegal wildlife products to enter legitimate supply chains.

### Livelihoods & Cultural Heritage

Communities in different regions may depend economically or culturally on animal based material industries.

### Environmental Change

Climate change and ecosystem disruption can alter species distributions and contribute to invasive species challenges.

---

## Stakeholder Tensions

The project analyzes competing priorities among multiple stakeholders.

### Luxury Brands

Seek differentiation, craftsmanship, profitability, and brand prestige while facing increasing sustainability expectations.

### Conservation Organizations

Prioritize biodiversity protection and ecosystem integrity.

### Governments

Must balance environmental commitments, regulation, economic activity, employment, and rural livelihoods.

### Indigenous & Local Communities

May depend on traditional livelihoods and possess cultural relationships with animal based materials.

### Consumers

Increasingly express sustainability concerns while continuing to purchase products associated with scarcity and status.

### Regulators

Must distinguish legal trade from illegal wildlife trafficking while maintaining workable international trade systems.

These conflicting objectives make the problem difficult to solve through any single intervention.

---

## Enterprise Risk Analysis

The project identifies several categories of enterprise exposure.

### Regulatory Risk

Weak or inconsistent enforcement can expose companies to:

• Product seizures  
• Fines  
• Shipment delays  
• Compliance failures  

### Reputational Risk

Companies face potential brand damage associated with:

• Greenwashing  
• Animal welfare concerns  
• Biodiversity impacts  
• Questionable sourcing claims  

### Supply Chain Risk

Fragmented global sourcing networks can create:

• Audit costs  
• Supply disruptions  
• Verification challenges  
• Traceability problems  

### Biodiversity Risk

Continued dependence on threatened species can increase long term environmental and regulatory exposure.

---

## Enterprise Response 1: Material Substitution

One potential strategy is reducing dependence on traditional exotic animal skins by increasing the use of alternative materials.

Potential alternatives include:

• Bio based materials  
• Recycled composites  
• Engineered materials  
• Next generation non animal materials  

The strategic challenge is to position these materials as an evolution of luxury rather than as a lower quality substitute.

---

## Enterprise Response 2: Traceability & Verification

The project proposes strengthening end to end traceability across exotic leather supply chains.

Potential mechanisms include:

• Third party audits  
• Chain of custody documentation  
• Species identification  
• Geographic source verification  
• DNA testing  
• Tamper resistant tagging  
• Digital records  
• Certification systems  

Improved traceability can help firms reduce information asymmetry and strengthen confidence in sustainability claims.

---

## Enterprise Response 3: Invasive Species Sourcing

The project also examines whether invasive species can become a responsible source of luxury materials when their removal contributes to ecosystem recovery.

Examples considered include:

• Burmese pythons in Florida  
• Lionfish in Caribbean ecosystems  

This approach attempts to align commercial incentives with biodiversity management.

Rather than sourcing from endangered or vulnerable populations, enterprises could potentially use species whose controlled removal provides ecological benefits.

---

## Strategic Tradeoffs

The project does not treat invasive species sourcing as a perfect solution.

Potential limitations include:

• Limited scalability  
• Geographic concentration of invasive species  
• Variable material quality  
• Processing costs  
• Traceability requirements  
• Risk of encouraging continued demand for exotic materials  
• Possibility of unintended market incentives  
• Community livelihood implications  

Therefore, invasive species sourcing is evaluated as a potential transitional strategy rather than a complete long term solution.

---

## Long Term Sustainability Strategy

The analysis recommends a layered approach.

### Near Term

Use carefully controlled invasive species sourcing where ecological benefits can be independently verified.

### Medium Term

Strengthen traceability, verification, auditing, and chain of custody systems.

### Long Term

Reduce dependence on threatened animal species by shifting luxury value toward innovation, craftsmanship, material performance, and sustainable alternatives.

The strategic objective is to preserve the attributes customers associate with luxury while reducing biodiversity related risk.
